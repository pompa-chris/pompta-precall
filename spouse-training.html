Section 1

Why Spouse Matters

Let's be honest about why spouse attendance matters — and how to frame it externally.

What We Know Internally

1. Decision-Making Power — Most people don't make $9,000–$15,000 decisions alone. Without the spouse, the lead hears everything but has to "sell" the spouse afterward. The spouse gets a 5-minute summary of an hour-long conversation. Questions don't get answered, doubts build up, and the decision gets delayed or killed.

2. Financial Clarity — When both partners are on the call, they can discuss it in real-time. No "let me talk to my husband/wife and get back to you" loops.

3. Support System Success — Clients who have spouse support have better outcomes: accountability partner, help with lifestyle changes, less resistance later in the program.

4. Preventing Future Objections — If the spouse isn't involved from the start, they may question legitimacy later. They weren't sold, so they become a barrier.

What We Tell the Lead
"This is one of the most important conversations you're going to have about your health. It affects your whole household — your health, your family, your finances, your future quality of life. This is a family decision, not just an individual one."
"We want your spouse to understand the journey you're about to go on. They'll be your biggest support system. When they hear it directly from {HAName}, they can be an advocate instead of a skeptic."
"Dr. Pompa requires the spouse to be on the call because we've seen the best transformations happen when the whole household is aligned."
"We don't want you to have to try to explain an hour-long conversation in 10 minutes. If you both hear it together, there's no confusion about next steps or commitments."
Bottom Line
We want a decision made on the call. That requires both decision-makers present.
Section 2

The Approach: Ask Before You Tell

Key Insight
Before you explain ANY of the reasons why spouse should attend, ask questions first. Let the client reveal the REAL objection, then tailor your response. You might only need ONE point instead of dumping all four.
The Problem with "Tell First"

When you lead with a list of reasons why the spouse should be there, the client may:

  • Not hear you — they're already forming their counter-argument
  • Think "why is this such a big deal? Is this a million dollars?"
  • Feel lectured, not heard
  • Dig in harder on their "no"
The "Ask First" Approach

Step 1: Soft open — Don't demand. Ask casually:

"Obviously you've got the Zoom information and everything — should I also send that information to your husband?"

Step 2: If they push back, go to discovery — Don't argue. Ask:

"Does he really have a good understanding of what's going on with you health-wise? What's his perspective on that?"

This is the best opening question. It gets them talking about the REAL situation — and 90% of the time, the real objection surfaces here.

Step 3: Listen, then give ONE tailored point — Based on what they told you, pick the single most relevant reason. You don't need all four reasons — just the one that addresses their actual concern.

Example Flow
You:"Should I send the Zoom info to your husband too?"
Client:"He'll probably be working."
You:"Got it. Does he have a solid understanding of what you're dealing with health-wise?"
Client:"I mean... he knows I haven't been feeling well. I can't really do fun stuff or show up the way he'd want."
You:"It sounds like this is really affecting more than just you — it's affecting him too. What we find is when both partners sit down on this call together, they can see the whole picture and actually focus on getting to the other side of these health issues. I think that's exactly why we need him there."
Notice: One tailored point ("it's affecting him too") landed harder than a list of four generic reasons would have. The client told you the angle — you just reflected it back.
Remember
What you say is a lie. What THEY say is the truth. If you tell them "your husband should be there because X, Y, Z" — they'll argue. If you ask questions until THEY say "yeah, he probably should understand what I'm going through" — that's real buy-in.
Section 3

The Assumptive Frame

Never ask a yes/no question about spouse attendance. Instead, assume they're attending and ask HOW.

Do (Assumptive)
"For your husband — either way works for us — is he going to be with you physically on the Zoom, or will he be joining virtually from somewhere else?"

This gives only two options: physically together or virtually separate. Both = attending.

Don't (Yes/No)
"Will your spouse be joining the call?"

This gives them a free "no" — and most will take it.

Alternate Soft Open

Another effective opener (works well for texters transitioning to calls):

"Should I send the Zoom link to your husband too, or will you be on the same device?"

This is gentler but still assumes attendance. It gives them an action-oriented choice, not a yes/no.

Section 4

The Three No's Framework

Your framework for handling spouse pushback. You get three attempts before digging deeper or making exceptions.

1
Assumptive Rebook

Lead says: "My spouse can't make it."

"No problem! Let's get you rebooked for a time when they can be there. What day works for both of you?"

Goal: Assume they'll reschedule. Don't ask permission — offer the solution.

2
Emphasize Importance

Lead says: "No, they're really too busy."

"I hear you. It is really important for us to have the spouse on — it's part of Dr. Pompa's protocol. They can really be supportive of your journey, and who knows, they may even have something with their own health they want to discuss in the future. Can we look at a time that might work for both of you?"

Goal: Reinforce this isn't optional while keeping it collaborative.

3
Discovery Mode

Lead says: "No, they just can't."

"Okay, walk me through that. What's coming up for you around this?"

Goal: Stop pushing and start listening. Find out what's really going on. Then use the appropriate talk track below.

Section 5

Talk Tracks for Common Objections

After reaching Discovery Mode (No #3), listen to the real objection, then use the matching talk track below.

"They're too busy"

Discovery:

"Tell me more about that. Are they working during all the times available, or is there something else going on?"

Response if scheduling issue:

"I totally understand — schedules are crazy. {HAName} has some evening and early morning availability. Can we find about an hour that works for both of you?"

Response if deeper:

"Is it that they're busy, or are they not fully on board with you exploring this?"
"They don't think they need to be involved"
"That's actually really common. Here's the thing though — this is going to affect them whether they realize it or not. Lifestyle changes, kitchen overhauls, understanding what you're going through. When they hear it directly from {HAName}, they become part of your support system instead of feeling left out. It's only about an hour. Would they be willing to just listen in?"
"They're skeptical"
"Perfect! {HAName} loves skeptics. Seriously. They'd rather answer tough questions directly than have your spouse wondering on the sidelines. Tell them to bring their doubts. {HAName} can handle it. Analytical people usually leave the call going 'okay, that actually makes sense.' Let's find a time when they can join."
"They think this is a scam"
"Ha, I get it. They've probably watched you try things that didn't work, and they're protective. That's actually a good thing — they care about you. What I'd say is: have them join the call and ask their hardest questions directly. {HAName} isn't going to pressure anyone. If it doesn't make sense, they'll say so. But at least your spouse will hear it firsthand instead of filtering it through their doubts."
"I make my own health decisions"
"I totally respect that. And you're absolutely the one making the decision about your health. The reason we ask for spouse involvement isn't to give them veto power — it's because this program requires real lifestyle changes. Having them understand what you're doing, and why, makes your success rate much higher. They become your accountability partner. Does that make sense?"
"We're not really together / separating"
"I appreciate you sharing that with me. In that case, we can absolutely proceed without them. Is there someone else in your life who's part of your support system — a parent, sibling, close friend — who might want to join? If not, that's okay too. Just make sure you're in a quiet place where you can focus for about an hour."
Action: Note this in CRM, proceed with single booking.
"We don't make financial decisions together"
"Got it. That's perfectly fine. We still recommend having them hear the health side of things — understanding what you're going through, the approach, the lifestyle changes. But if you're telling me they're truly not involved in any part of this decision, I can note that and we can proceed."
Action: If they confirm, note in CRM and proceed.
"They already know everything about my health journey"
"That's great that they're already involved! In that case, the call is a perfect next step — they can hear directly from {HAName} about what's happening at the cellular level. Knowing your journey is different from understanding the science. Would they be open to joining?"
"I already talked to them and they said it's fine"
"That's awesome that you've talked about it! Here's the thing though — giving you permission is different from really understanding. {HAName} is going to cover a lot in about an hour. Having them hear it directly means you won't have to try to explain it all afterward. Plus, they might have questions of their own. Can we find a time when they're available?"
Section 6

The #1 Real Objection

By Far the Most Common
The husband is tired of the wife spending money on health things that don't work. She's tried 20 things in 5 years, spent $10,000+, and nothing has worked. He's done hearing about it.
Surface It Directly

If they don't volunteer this, ask:

"From his perspective — is it that you've tried a lot of things that haven't really worked, and he doesn't want you to spend any more money? Is that the case?"

They'll usually say yes. Then redirect:

"Okay — so does he actually understand WHY those things didn't work for you? Because when he comes on the call, he gets to learn what you've actually been going through, how it's affecting your body, what our treatment looks like, how it would help you, AND why what you tried before didn't work. That's all covered in about an hour."
Other Common Root Causes

"He only trusts regular doctors"

"Isn't that exactly why we'd want him there? We can explain things at a scientific level that analytical people really respond to. Do you think he'd have questions?"

"He's not into health stuff"

"This affects him too — schedules, meals, routines. When he understands what's happening, he becomes your support system instead of a skeptic."

"I make my own decisions"

Move to the Financial Decision Closer (Section 8).

Section 7

Caller Objection Ladder

3–4 attempts max. 5–10 minutes ceiling. Then move to the Financial Closer or let it go.

Attempt 1: Rebook Offer
"I hear you. Would it be better if we found a day and time that worked for both of you, so you could be together for it?"
Attempt 2: Importance + Support Framing
"The consultation is about an hour long, full of detailed recommendations and important findings about your root cause. It's hard to recap all that later. We just want you to feel supported, not like you're carrying this alone."
Attempt 3: Discovery / Perspective Question
"What's his perspective on what's going on with your health? Does he actually understand what you've been dealing with?"
Attempt 4: "Just Listen In" Downgrade
"Could he at least just listen in for a few minutes? He doesn't have to be on camera or all involved. Just there to hear a bit and support you."

This is the lowest-commitment ask. Most people will agree to "a few minutes."

Section 8

The Financial Decision Closer

Last Resort Only
Use this after 3–4 failed attempts when the spouse truly won't attend. This is your white-flag moment.
"I hear you. Everyone's situation is different. If we were to find that we're able to help you and make a recommendation — do you feel that you'd be able to move forward with the treatment process and make those decisions about your health, including financial decisions, without him?"
If They Say YES
  • Proceed with the appointment
  • Tag the HA with context: "She said she has the money, she can make the decision independently, she sounds confident. We went back and forth. It's now up to your discretion."
  • They may be lying or overconfident, but you've done your job
If They Say NO
  • This confirms spouse IS needed
  • Offer to reschedule for a time when both can attend
  • If they can't find a time, discuss cancellation
Section 9

The "Wall" Phenomenon

Expect This 1–2 Times Per Week
When you mention the spouse joining the call, the client (usually women) goes into rapid-fire "no no no no no" mode. They won't let you finish a sentence.
What's Happening
  • They may have a difficult marriage or prefer to keep health decisions secret
  • They've made up their mind before you even asked
  • No amount of logic will change it in that moment — not even Dr. Pompa himself could
How to Handle It
  1. Don't fight the wall. You will not win by talking louder or repeating your points.
  2. Try ONE discovery question: "Okay, is this kind of a secret mission?" — sometimes humor disarms them.
  3. Offer to reschedule if timing is the issue.
  4. If they're adamant after genuine effort — let it go. It's better they show up solo than not at all.
  5. Note it clearly in CRM so the HA knows spouse will not be present and why.
At a certain point, pushing harder becomes counterproductive. If they're at a firm "no" and you've genuinely tried, proceed with the appointment. A solo show is better than a no-show.
Mindset

You need to sound confident and firm (not like a customer service agent), but there's a line. Preserve the appointment. Your job is genuine effort, not winning every time.

Section 10

Using Testimonials for Spouse Buy-In

The Testimonials Tool

After the spouse conversation, text the testimonials link. Say:

"I'm going to send you something — our testimonials page has real stories from people with conditions similar to yours. You can search by symptom. Maybe show one of these videos to your husband — sometimes hearing someone else's story makes it click."

Why it works:

  • People are more likely to show a VIDEO to their spouse than try to explain it themselves
  • This gives the spouse a "third party" voice — not just their partner asking them to come
When to Send It
  • During the spouse conversation as a supporting tool
  • At the end of the call as part of your positive close
  • In a follow-up text if you're still working on spouse buy-in
Section 11

When to Make Exceptions

After the Three No's, if you've done discovery and one of these situations applies, you can proceed without spouse.

Acceptable Exceptions
  1. Single / Divorced / Widowed — Confirm relationship status, proceed
  2. Separated / Going through divorce — Proceed, note in CRM
  3. Truly independent finances — Both parties confirmed, note in CRM
  4. Spouse medically unable — Proceed with empathy
  5. Domestic situation concerns — Use judgment, prioritize safety
How to Proceed
  1. Note the exception clearly in CRM
  2. Inform the HA in advance
  3. Ask if there's another support person who could join
  4. Confirm the lead will be in a quiet, focused environment
What to Tell the HA

Include in your handoff note:

"Spouse not attending — [reason]. Lead confirmed they [make decisions independently / are separated / single]. No other support person available. Lead seems [ready/hesitant/needs extra care]."
Post-Call Communication Templates

Spouse confirmed:

@{HAName} — {ClientName} is good to go. NTA done, kit received, spouse {SpouseName} will be joining. Just needs to [finish urine test / test Zoom link].

Spouse NOT attending:

@{HAName} — {ClientName}: spouse will NOT be attending. She says she makes financial decisions independently, went back and forth 3-4 times, sounds confident. Your discretion on whether to proceed or cancel.
